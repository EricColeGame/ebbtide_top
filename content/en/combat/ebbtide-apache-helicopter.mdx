## How the Ebbtide Apache Helicopter Fits the Hybrid RTS Loop

Ebbtide is doing something unusual by mixing real-time strategy command with hands-on survival. You can drop into a survivor's boots and shoot, or you can zoom out and manage squads, vehicles, and fire support. That dual identity is exactly where the Ebbtide Apache helicopter becomes interesting.

In a pure RTS, an attack helicopter is a simple damage unit. In a hybrid game, it is also a *camera*. Gunships fly above the street grid, which means they can reveal horde movement, spot breaches in your rooftop defenses, and buy time for an evacuation convoy. If the game leans into that, air units will be valued as much for information as for firepower.

Here is how air support typically slots into a loop like this:

- **Recon first.** Use the airframe to find where the horde is massing before you commit ground squads.
- **Shape the fight.** Rotary cannons and rockets thin dense clusters so your infantry isn't overrun.
- **Cover the retreat.** Air support is at its best when civilians or wounded squads need a corridor.
- **Preserve the asset.** Losing a helicopter is a strategic loss, not a minor inconvenience.

Community reports from the comment threads compare the game's feel to other zombie-RTS hybrids, and that comparison is fair in one respect: the tension comes from choosing between saving the city and saving your best units.

If you can't see the horde, use air. If you can see it and it's already touching your line, use artillery or armor. Rotating between those two responses is the core skill of commanding in a hybrid RTS.

### Micro Tips That Apply to Any Gunship

- **Strafe, don't circle.** Wide passes keep you out of concentrated return fire.
- **Attack from the sun side of the street.** Elevation plus angle equals survivability.
- **Set a rally point.** If your airframe goes down, your ground squads should already know where to fall back.
- **Bank cooldowns early.** The Ebbtide Apache helicopter, like any fire-support asset, is most valuable in the opening minute of a wave, not the last ten seconds.
